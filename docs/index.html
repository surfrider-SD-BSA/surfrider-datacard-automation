Surfrider Data Cards

Paper beach cleanup data cards, turned into the chapter’s spreadsheet.

Privacy policy · How this app uses your Google data

Volunteers record debris counts on paper data cards during a beach cleanup. Afterwards somebody has to type every value into a spreadsheet by hand — several hours of transcription for a large event, with errors that are hard to catch later.

Surfrider Data Cards reads the cards instead. You give it a scanned PDF of the stack. It finds each card, counts the tally marks, reads the handwritten numbers, shows you a cropped picture of every value it is unsure about so you can correct it, and hands back a finished spreadsheet in the standard Surfrider data entry format.

It is built for Surfrider Foundation volunteer chapters, and was developed and tested by the San Diego Chapter (CH54).

Where it runs

  • In a browser. The web tool reads the scan on your own computer. The scan is not uploaded — there is no server of ours for it to be uploaded to.
  • On an iPhone. The iOS app does the same reading on the device, offline.

Why the app asks to sign in to Google

Chapters keep their scans in a shared Google Drive folder. Rather than making a volunteer download a scan to their laptop and drag it in, the app can open it straight out of that folder — you press Choose from Google Drive, sign in with your own Google account, and pick the scan.

What that sign-in grants, exactly:

  • The app requests one scope, https://www.googleapis.com/auth/drive.file. That grants access to the individual files you pick in Google's own file picker and to nothing else in your Drive. It cannot list your folders, and it cannot open a file you did not choose.
  • The scan is downloaded from a folder that already holds it. Nothing about a card is uploaded to Google, and nothing is sent to us — we run no server.
  • When the app writes the finished spreadsheet back, it writes it to the same Drive folder the scan came from, and only because you asked it to.
  • The access token is held in memory while the app is open and is written to no file. Quitting the app ends the session.

All of this is set out in full in the privacy policy.

The broader drive.readonly scope would have been simpler to build against and is deliberately not used: it would mean asking a beach cleanup volunteer to grant a transcription tool the run of their entire Drive.

This feature is off unless a chapter configures it. A build made without a Google project has no Drive button and makes no network request of any kind.

What it is honest about

Deciding whether a box has writing in it is reliable. Reading what it says is not. The tool treats those differently: locating the writing is what it is built on, and the handwriting reading is offered as a first guess to check, with a picture of the original beside it. The measurements behind that — including how often the reader is wrong — are published in the repository rather than summarised away.
